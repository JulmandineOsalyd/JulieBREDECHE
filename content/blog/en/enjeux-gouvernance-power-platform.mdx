Someone builds an automation to save time. It works well, and their whole team uses it every day. Then that person leaves the company, and the automation stops working.

This happens more often than you would think. The IT department had never heard of this "tool", even though an entire team depended on it.

---

## 1. Power Platform Without Governance Is an Open Playground

By default, Power Platform is open: any user with a Microsoft 365 license can create apps and flows, unless the company puts restrictions in place.

Without a framework, I often see the same things:

- Power Automate flows pile up in the default environment
- Automations run with **personal credentials**
- Nothing is documented and the IT department doesn't know these tools exist

> **The problem isn't that business teams create automations. It's that nobody knows what they have created.**

---

## 2. A New Paradigm: Business Teams Become "Developers"

With Power Platform, **citizen developers** are emerging. These are operational teams who know the need better than anyone. They build fast and no longer depend on the IT department.

But this is not classic IT governance. The table shows the gap:

| | Traditional IT project management | Citizen developer project |
|---|---|---|
| Who builds | IT, on request | Business teams, on their own |
| Lead time | Weeks or months | Immediate, no waiting queue |
| Validation | Before going live | Often none |
| Owner | An identified team | A single person |

Business teams build fast, alone and without validation. The question is what the IT department needs to control, without stopping business teams from taking initiative.

---

## 3. New Challenges to Master

I see four major challenges linked to the rise of Power Platform and citizen developers.

### Challenge 01: Avoid Shadow IT

How do you get **visibility into what citizen developers create**, without taking away their autonomy? A ban would only move usage out of the IT department's sight.

### Challenge 02: Limit Security Gaps

Flows can be connected to critical systems without any security review. Without a DLP policy or clear rules, **a poorly configured flow can expose sensitive data**.

### Challenge 03: Control Capacity and Copilot Credits

What citizen developers create gradually consumes Dataverse storage, which is shared across the whole tenant. AI also adds a consumption of **Copilot credits** (see [Copilot Studio billing](/blog/modele-facturation-copilot-studio)). Without monitoring, the IT department risks restrictions or an unexpected bill.

### Challenge 04: Reduce Technical Debt

Critical apps are built quickly, without documentation, and sometimes rely on a single citizen developer. When that person leaves the company, nobody knows how to maintain them and **the business ends up paying the price**.

---

## 4. Points of Caution for the IT Department

Before building anything, here are five traps to avoid:

- **Stepping in too late**, when many apps already exist and catching up becomes expensive
- **Choosing to block**, because shadow IT then moves out of the IT department's view
- **Neglecting citizen developer training**, because without explanations, nobody knows what is allowed
- **Leaving monitoring aside**, when governance is something you define over time
- **Underestimating Copilot Studio**, whose agents create new usage and call for dedicated rules, on top of those for apps and flows

---

## My Conclusion

Citizen developers bring speed and autonomy, but without a framework these strengths become risks for the IT department. Before choosing tools, the first decision is to determine who owns the framework and the support for business teams.
